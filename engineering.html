Problem solving

Protecting the methanol injection pumps

The main methanol pump sent far more flow than the downstream pump that feeds the cryogenic injection points could handle, putting it at risk of damage.

I recommended a recirculation line on the main pump to return part of the flow to the tank, reducing the flow and pressure on the second pump.

Reported to my manager and escalated to the project and plant managers.

The design used nitrogen to keep lubricating oil flowing to the turbo expander if power was lost, but the contractor's isometric drawings had left it out.

I walked the contractor through the P&ID and isometrics and made sure it was installed.

Start-up procedures

Getting the air out before the gas goes in

Wrote the nitrogen purge and gas start-up procedures for two new 12-inch customer pipelines, approved by our Process Engineering Manager.

See the detail

Before a new pipeline carries natural gas, the air inside has to go. Oxygen and gas must never meet in a pipe. The two interconnections were 450 m and 80 m long.

  • Nitrogen first. Calculated the nitrogen each line needed (88.7 m³ and 39.9 m³), charged at no more than 7 bar, to sweep the air out.
  • A clear finish line. Purge until oxygen at the far end reads 2% or less, pack the line to 2 barg, then check every flange and joint for leaks before anyone touches gas.
  • Then the gas. Sweep the nitrogen out with lean gas until methane reads 80% three times, three minutes apart, and bring the line up to 50 barg. I estimated the gas needed and the time to pressurise: under half an hour per line.
  • Every valve accounted for. Wrote the line-up valve by valve for every skid (distribution headers, flow control, metering, emergency shutdown) and marked up a dedicated P&ID, colour-coded open and closed, so the field team could check the alignment at a glance.
  • Ready for the day. Set out the start-up organisation across both stations, the materials list, the task risk assessment and the emergency response plan, plus simple rules like starting in daylight and telling nearby communities first.
Hydraulic study

Making sure the furthest hydrant still works

Modelled a gas plant's fire suppression network in INPLANT and found what the pump and pipework need to reach the most remote hydrant.

See the detail

A fire system is only as strong as its most remote hydrant. I built a steady-state hydraulic model and ran four scenarios, moving the hydrant monitor around the layout.

  • The critical case was the monitor at the end of the longest run. To deliver 8 barg at 3,000 L/min there, the pump must discharge at least 146 psig.
  • The pipe size matters. The 6-inch main runs at 2.69 m/s, under the NFPA limit of 3 m/s. Anything narrower would choke it.
  • My recommendations: no pipe under 6 inches, a pump curve meeting NFPA 25, a closed ring main with isolation valves, pressure gauges at the far ends, vents at high points and drains at low points.
Performance reporting

Measuring the pipeline honestly

Adapted OEE into Overall Operations Effectiveness for a gas pipeline and showed where performance was really being lost.

See the detail

OEE was built for machines, so I adapted it into Overall Operations Effectiveness (OOE) for a whole gas pipeline, with a daily, monthly and yearly Excel report tracking availability, performance and gas quality.

  • The pipeline was 100% available and delivered 45.4 MMscf/d to the power plant against 47.7 MMscf/d demanded (95% performance) at 100% gas quality.
  • Overall effectiveness fell to 65% only because upstream supply was there 68% of the time.
  • The fix wasn't in our pipeline. It was upstream, and the report gave the team the numbers to show it.

Document control

One name for everything

Wrote the procedure that gives every document, piece of equipment, pipe and instrument a single, unmistakable code.

See the detail
